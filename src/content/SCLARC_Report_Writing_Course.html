SCLARC Report Writing

A step-by-step course for trainees & training clients · Facilitated by Krystel Edmonds-Biglow, Psy.D.

What this course does. It shows you how to write a Regional Center (SCLARC) psychological evaluation, from the first interview to the report you turn in. It covers how to write, how to organize the report, how to reason through the DSM-5-TR, how to use the Autism and Intellectual Disability templates, and it includes three de-identified sample reports. Read it in order the first time. After that, go straight to the part you need.
How to use it. Every section starts with the main point in one line. The reference sheets, templates, and checklist are here to keep open while you write. The AI prompts are for people using Bastion. If you use Reverb, it builds the mapping for you and you do not need the prompts. Whether you use AI or not, the templates are what you follow. Check every fact against your own data, and proofread the whole report before you turn it in.

1 · The report and the eligibility question

In short. Every Regional Center report has one job: to answer one question clearly, in the Diagnostic Impressions section.

Regional Centers hire licensed psychologists to decide whether an applicant has a qualifying developmental disability under California’s Lanterman Act. Your report needs to answer one question head-on: does the applicant have a qualifying condition (intellectual disability and/or autism spectrum disorder) that started before age 18, is likely to last, and gets in the way of daily life in a real way? The Regional Center makes the final eligibility decision, not you. Your job is to give their team everything they need to make it.

The SCLARC eligibility team reads these reports. Applicants, families, attorneys, and courts may read them too. A report that does not meet SCLARC’s standards gets sent back (“kicked back”). This course is based on the SCLARC Standards for Psychological Evaluations, the DSM-5-TR, and the problems that show up most often in reports that get returned.

2 · The six voice principles

In short. Every paragraph in every report obeys six principles: name, tense, show, teach, attribute, affirm.
PrincipleWhat it means
NameUse the client’s name in every paragraph. The client isn’t at the eligibility meeting — the report is. Replace “the client” everywhere; this alone raises warmth.
TensePresent tense, active voice. “Lawrence demonstrates,” not “demonstrated.” Present tense keeps the reader with the person as they are today.
ShowShow, don’t label. “Diego oriented toward the examiner but did not initiate joint attention” — not “Diego is socially awkward.” Specific behavior in specific context.
TeachTeach the measure, then report scores. Open each measure paragraph by naming what the measure is for; then the scores; then the interpretation.
AttributeAttribute the informant. Every reported behavior carries a source: (per maternal report on the ADI-R), (observed during ADOS-2 Task 4). A reviewer can trace evidence to its source.
AffirmNeurodiversity-affirming language. “Presents with,” “demonstrates,” “experiences” — not “suffers from.” Diagnoses direct treatment; they are not verdicts about the person.

4 · Intake & clinical interview

In short. The more specific you get in the interview, the more specific your report can be. A thin interview leaves you with a thin report.

The 13 intake domains (Reverb)

  • 1 Presenting concern — what brings you in; a bad day vs. a good day; who else is worried; what’s been tried.
  • 2 Family & developmental history — pregnancy, delivery, early temperament; motor/language/social/play milestones with ages.
  • 3 Behavior — walk through a typical meltdown start to finish; triggers; what helps recovery; repetitive behaviors.
  • 4 Social communication — starting conversations, reading emotions, jokes/sarcasm, making and keeping friends.
  • 5 Play & interests — what they choose to play with; intense or fixated interests and how much of the day.
  • 6 RRB / sensory — B1 stereotyped movements; B2 insistence on sameness; B3 restricted interests; B4 sensory reactivity.
  • 7 Adaptive — daily living, self-care, community, executive function.
  • 8 Psychiatric history — prior evaluations, therapy, medication, hospitalizations.
  • 9 Trauma & adverse experiences — ask for every client; central for foster-care/removed youth.
  • 10 Educational history — every school, special-education services, performance, teacher observations.
  • 11 Medical — conditions, medications, hearing/vision, neurological.
  • 12 Legal — juvenile justice, custody, pending matters.
  • 13 Interventions to date — named services, duration, provider, family’s perception of what helped.
Three cross-setting probes (these later substantiate cross-context stability): (1) Where is the behavior most, and least, intense? (2) Who must be present for it to happen, and who can make it stop? (3) Is it different when the caregiver changes — mom, dad, foster caregiver, teacher, grandparent?

The full SCLARC Clinical Interview Protocol expands these into demographic data, living situation, circle of support, daily routine, fixated interests and their social impact, attachment to objects, impact across settings, accommodations, and the remaining SCLARC domains. Document with attention to discrepancies and corroboration across sources.

5 · Behavioral observations & test results

In short. Observations put the reader in the room; test paragraphs teach the measure before the scores; the ADOS-2 write-up is the anchor of every ASD report.

Behavioral observations — before & after

Earlier draft
“Lawrence was cooperative and quiet. Some difficulty with eye contact. Restricted affect.”
Refined draft
Lawrence arrived dressed neatly, hands in his pockets, and remained standing near the door for about thirty seconds before his foster mother gestured him toward the chair… His affect was constricted and did not shift meaningfully across content, even when the discussion turned to the death of his biological mother.

What made it better: the name in every sentence, present tense, swapping “quiet” for the actual behavior, describing his affect by what did not change, and quoting the restricted interest as it happened.

The ADOS-2 deep dive

The ADOS-2 section is the heart of every autism report. If it is weak, the rest of the report cannot make up for it. Start with the required teaching paragraph that says what the measure does, then tie every score to the specific tasks where you saw the behavior.

Teaching-paragraph template. “The Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2), Module [X], is a standardized, semi-structured observational assessment designed to elicit behaviors relevant to Autism Spectrum Disorder. It consists of [N] activities that provide opportunities to observe reciprocal social interaction, social communication, imaginative use of materials, and restricted or repetitive behaviors. Scores combine into Social Affect (SA) and Restricted and Repetitive Behavior (RRB) domain totals, yielding a calibrated severity score interpreted against DSM-5-TR ASD criteria, with attention to the pattern across tasks rather than any single item.”
Earlier draft
“Diego’s Social Affect score fell in the High range. His RRB score fell in the High range.”
Refined draft
Diego’s Social Affect calibrated severity score fell in the High range, driven by limited spontaneous joint attention during Task 5 (Free Play), reduced modulation of facial expression during shared-enjoyment tasks (Tasks 3 and 6), and minimal gesture throughout. His RRB score fell in the High range, driven by sustained visual inspection of the toy vehicles during Task 5 and repetitive vocalizations during transitions.

6 · DSM-5-TR criteria reference sheets

In short. One reference per diagnosis. Confirm criteria while you write.
F84.0 — Autism Spectrum Disorder

A — Persistent deficits in social communication/interaction across contexts: A1 reciprocity · A2 nonverbal · A3 relationships (all three).
B — Restricted, repetitive patterns; at least 2 of 4: B1 stereotyped · B2 sameness · B3 restricted interests · B4 sensory.
C — Present in early developmental period. D — Clinically significant impairment. E — Not better explained by ID/GDD.
Specifiers: with/without intellectual impairment; with/without language impairment; associated medical/genetic condition; with/without catatonia. Severity Level 1–3 for each of A and B.

F70–F73 — Intellectual Disabilities

A — Deficits in intellectual functions, confirmed by clinical assessment and standardized testing. B — Deficits in adaptive functioning across settings (severity set by adaptive functioning, not IQ). C — Onset in the developmental period.
Codes: F70 Mild · F71 Moderate · F72 Severe · F73 Profound.

F88 / F89 — Other Specified / Unspecified NDD

F88 — NDD features causing significant impairment but not meeting full criteria for a specific NDD; specify the reason (name what is present, what threshold isn’t met, why F88).
F89 — Same, but the clinician chooses not to specify — usually because information is insufficient or confounders preclude it. The formulation must name the confounders.

F90.x — ADHD · F43.10/F43.9 — Trauma · F94.1/F94.2 — Attachment · F32.9 — Depressive

F90.x ADHD: A1 inattention (6+/5+ if 17+) ≥6 mo; A2 hyperactivity-impulsivity (6+/5+) ≥6 mo; B before age 12; C in ≥2 settings; D interference; E not better explained. Codes .0 inattentive · .1 hyperactive-impulsive · .2 combined.
F43.10 PTSD: exposure + intrusion + avoidance + negative cognition/mood + arousal, ≥1 month. F43.9 unspecified trauma/stressor.
F94.1 RAD (inhibited/withdrawn) · F94.2 DSED (disinhibited approach).
F32.9 unspecified depressive — name symptoms, impairment, and why unspecified.

7 · Trauma vs. ASD — foster-care differential

In short. When ASD-looking features appear in a trauma-exposed client, substantiate the diagnosis above and beyond the trauma. Four discriminators.

Every child who has been removed from home or is in foster care has been through trauma. So if you diagnose autism, your report has to show that the autism is there on top of the trauma, not just a reaction to it. And if you considered autism but did not diagnose it, the report has to say why. Either way, the Differential Considerations part is the first thing the Regional Center reader turns to.

DiscriminatorASD points…Trauma points…
(a) Developmental origin (Criterion C)Features present before the identifiable trauma; anchored to ADI-R Age of Onset, Early Start, or pre-removal records.Onset named after an identifiable stressor.
(b) Cross-context stabilitySame intensity across all settings and caregivers, stable across affective loading.Intensifies in trauma-associated contexts, reduces in safe ones.
(c) Nature of the interestIntrinsic, pattern-based (trains, cataloguing, spinning, numeric sequencing).Identity- or safety-organized (family exposure, threat-associated content).
(d) Response to safety / time-courseFeatures persist across months/years in stable placement.Hypervigilance, startle, constricted affect reduce with felt safety over time.
How to write the verdict. Say whether F84.0 is met, not met, or deferred, and go through all four discriminators one by one, pointing to what this client’s own data shows for each. Write it so anyone reading later can follow your reasoning. If the person who gave the history did not see the client’s early years, say so plainly in the report, and note that Criterion C cannot rest on that ADI-R.

9 · Using AI (Bastion) — prompts, mapping & accuracy

Read this first. AI can help you draft and format. It is not the clinician and it never makes the clinical decisions. If you use Bastion, the prompts below build your criterion map. If you use Reverb, it builds the map for you and you do not need these prompts. Either way, the templates in this section are what you follow. The AI will not write “Met” or “Not Met” for you; you add that yourself. Check every score, quote, and fact against your own data, and proofread the whole report before it goes to your supervisor.

The three-step Bastion workflow

  • Step 1 — Integrative summary. Paste your report draft (background + test data), then the integrative-summary prompt. The AI returns a one-paragraph summary of how the client meets criteria, with scores.
  • Step 2 — Code + specifiers. Ask the AI to write the DSM-5-TR code with the correct specifiers for your client.
  • Step 3 — Build the map. Paste the mapping template (below) and your report draft; the AI returns the full criterion map for your client. Then you add “Met / Not Met” under each criterion and check every line.

About this program

This is a 12-month doctoral practicum. It runs from September 2026 through August 2027. You will do psychotherapy and psychological and neuropsychological testing with children, teens, and adults from many backgrounds. A licensed psychologist supervises all of your work. You will also work with other people on the team, including registered psychological associates, psychological technicians and assistants, and other doctoral-level clinicians, who are there to help you. Our work is based on research, it respects each client’s culture, and it starts with knowing yourself.

What we expect

  • Only do work that fits your training, and only with a supervisor.
  • Finish notes in Ensora (TheraNest) within 48 hours. Finish the treatment plan and diagnosis the same day as the intake. Turn in testing reports within one week.
  • Make two attempts to reach a client before you stop trying, then let the Training Director know.
  • Protect client privacy (HIPAA), keep your communication professional, and bring up any clinical or ethical questions early in supervision.

Weeks 1 & 2 · Onboarding & Workflow

In short. Get set up: learn who we are, how we work, and the systems you will use.

Welcome to Consulting Collective. This month the training is self-paced, so review all the modules on your own time before our first in-person meeting. Start dates and workdays are different for each trainee, based on the days you can complete your practicum hours. If your start date is different from the group, email the Training Director with the date you plan to begin and we will make a plan together.

Your role

You are a doctoral student in training. You provide services only with a licensed psychologist supervising you. You will also work with other clinicians on the team who are there to help. Your supervisor makes the final call on diagnoses, test interpretation, reports, and feedback.

How the work flows

  • Testing referrals. Call the client, introduce yourself as part of the clinical team, and explain that you gather history and give the tests while your supervisor interprets the scores, makes the diagnosis, writes the report, and gives feedback. Do an intake, meet your supervisor to pick the measures, do the testing (some clients may need it online), then turn in a complete, proofread draft report.
  • Therapy clients. Introduce yourself as part of the clinical team, let them know services are starting and that they will get an Ensora (TheraNest) portal link, and reassure them that a supervisor reviews their care. Front-office staff check that the client is comfortable seeing a supervised trainee; clients who need a licensed provider are referred out. Add the client and send the portal link.
  • Insurance. Front-office staff verify insurance and send the paperwork to our billing partner. You are not responsible for insurance calls. Send anxious clients to the main office at (323) 379-3394.
  • Return calls and no-shows. Try to reach the client twice. If there is no answer, email the Training Director so we can note it.
  • Documentation. Notes within 48 hours; treatment plan and diagnosis the same day as the intake.

Week 3 · Evidence-Based Practice

In short. Use what the research shows actually helps clients.

Evidence-based practice means putting together three things: the best research, your clinical skill, and the client’s own characteristics, culture, and preferences. Years of research point to the same finding: the working relationship, called the alliance (the bond, plus agreeing on goals and on how you will get there), is one of the strongest signs a client will improve. So are empathy, asking for and using client feedback, agreeing on goals, and real positive regard. Adjust your approach to fit each person, and use simple progress measures so you notice early when someone is not getting better.

  • Norcross & Lambert — chapters 5, 7 & 8 (reading)
  • Norcross presentation (slides)
  • Book: Psychotherapy Relationships That Work (2nd ed.)
Do this. Write one short paragraph naming two parts of the relationship you want to strengthen this year, and one way you will measure the alliance or the client’s progress. Bring it to supervision.

Week 4 · Neurodevelopmental Disorders

In short. Build your current knowledge of autism and ADHD, and how to tell them apart.

ASD shows up as lasting differences in social communication and interaction, along with restricted, repetitive behaviors and interests, present from early in life. It is often caught later in girls and women because it can look milder or be masked. ADHD shows up as inattention and/or hyperactivity and impulsivity, with several symptoms before age 12 and in more than one setting. It often comes with trouble managing emotions and rejection-sensitive dysphoria. The two can happen together and can look alike on the surface, so gather information from several sources and always read behavior in context.

This week: read the DSM-5-TR section on Neurodevelopmental Disorders (pp. 31–88) and watch the videos below.

  • Neurodevelopmental disorders: sufficient & necessary causes
  • 10 Early Signs of Autism
  • What is ADHD?
  • ADHD and Emotional Dysregulation
  • ADHD in Women
  • An Overview of ADHD: the new diagnostic criteria
Do this. Write a half-page on how you would tell apart overlapping ADHD and ASD in a school-age child, and name two context or culture factors that could change how you read the behavior.

Week 5 · Comprehensive & Culturally Responsive Assessment

In short. Do a thorough, culturally fair evaluation with clients of any age.

Tests are not culture-free. A person’s level of acculturation, the language the test is given in, their access to education, their income and neighborhood, and their trauma history can all affect their scores, and can look like impairment when it is not. Good practice starts before you pick a test: get clear on the referral question, take a full history that includes context, choose measures with the right norms, and read the results in light of the whole person. Keep in mind that trauma can cause memory and attention problems that you have to tell apart from other causes.

  • Cultural Considerations for Cognitive Assessment (slides)
  • Creating an Antiracist Psychology (Professional Complicity in Assessment)
  • Posttraumatic Stress & Memory Complaints in Neuropsychological Settings
  • Ardila (2005): Cultural Values Underlying Psychometric Cognitive Testing
  • Webinar: Developmental Assessment — Gateway to Early Intervention
  • Webinar: The Autistic Experience Across the Lifespan (WPS)
Do this. Make a one-page pre-testing checklist of the culture and context factors you will always screen for, and note how each one could change how you read the results.

Week 6 · Social Justice, Multicultural Assessment & Ethics

In short. Culturally responsive, ethical work starts with looking at yourself.

Use Hays’s ADDRESSING framework (Age, Disabilities that are developmental or acquired, Religion, Ethnicity and race, Socioeconomic status, Sexual orientation, Indigenous heritage, National origin, Gender) to map both your client’s identities and your own, and to notice your own areas of privilege and bias. Remember that assessment sits inside larger systems: family, school, community, and society. Poverty, discrimination, and unfair systems affect how a client presents and what care they can get. Watch for biased interpretation, use the right norms, and make recommendations the family can actually use. Be ready to keep the relationship steady and stay true to your values when a client says something prejudiced.

  • Social Justice in Therapy (slides)
  • Cultural Sketch (ADDRESSING framework activity)
  • Multicultural Assessment and Ethical Practice
  • Semi-Structured Parent Interview Questions
  • Working with Families in Poverty
Do this. Review the Social Justice in Therapy slides, then fill out the Cultural Sketch for yourself. Be ready to talk in supervision about one way your own background could shape your clinical judgment.

Week 8 · Intake Sessions

In short. Get ready to run your first intakes with the right forms and measures.

A good intake gathers the main concern and its history: developmental, medical, family, social, educational, and legal history, plus risk, strengths, and the client’s goals. For minors, find out who has the legal right to consent (this matters when parents live in separate homes), and hold the first session with the child and their guardian. Explain consent, assent, what stays private and what does not, and how you protect their privacy. Send the intake packet and get it signed before the first session, read the Guide for Intake Session 1, and have your measures ready. Write your notes on time: the note within 48 hours, and the treatment plan and diagnosis the same day as the intake.

SCLARC Clinical Interview Guidelines — full protocol

Clinical Interview Protocol (Expanded): SCLARC and California Regional Center Evaluations. Use this to structure your intake so the interview covers everything the report will need. Document responses with attention to discrepancies, corroboration across sources, and relevance to SCLARC eligibility.

Center Evaluations

1. Demographic and Administrative Data

  • Confirm applicant’s name, DOB, age at evaluation, UCI, examiner and

supervisor credentials, referring Regional Center, Service

Coordinator, evaluation format, and total administration time.

2. Current Living Situation

  • Who currently resides in the home with the applicant? (List all

individuals, including relationship to applicant)

  • If a parent lives outside the home, when did this change occur?
  • Is custody shared? If so:
  • How much time does the applicant spend with each parent?
  • What is the regular visitation schedule?
  • What are the arrangements for holidays and school breaks?
  • Are there any recent or pending changes to the living arrangement?

3. Circle of Support

  • Which extended family members are involved in the applicant’s life

(grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins, others)?

  • What role does each person play in the applicant’s support system?
  • How frequently does the applicant have contact with each family

member?

  • What types of support (emotional, social, caregiving,

transportation, educational, recreational) do these individuals

provide?

  • Are there any significant relationships that contribute notably to

the applicant’s well-being or development?

  • Is the applicant involved in church, religious, spiritual, or

faith-based activities?

  • How frequently does the applicant participate?
  • Are there meaningful relationships or support received through

these activities?

  • Does the applicant participate in extracurricular activities

(sports, clubs, arts programs, community organizations, classes,

etc.)?

  • What is the frequency and level of involvement?
  • What relationships has the applicant developed through these

activities?

  • How do these activities contribute to the applicant’s social,

emotional, and recreational development?

4. School and Home Routine

  • School Schedule:
  • What time does school start and end?
  • How many hours does the applicant spend at school each day?
  • Home Schedule:
  • How many hours does the applicant spend at home outside of

school?

  • Morning Routine:
  • Describe the wake-up routine.
  • How does the applicant manage hygiene and dressing?
  • What is the breakfast routine?
  • How does the applicant prepare for school?
  • What level of independence and supervision is required?
  • After-School Routine:
  • How does the applicant approach homework?
  • Are therapies or services attended after school?
  • What extracurricular or leisure activities are typical?
  • How does the applicant interact with family members after

school?

  • What are the supervision arrangements?
  • Evening Routine:
  • Describe the dinner routine and family activities.
  • How much screen time or time spent on hobbies?
  • What is the bedtime routine and typical bedtime?
  • Weekend Routine:
  • What recreational activities, family outings, or community

involvement occur on weekends?

  • Does the applicant visit relatives or engage in special

interests/hobbies?

  • Responsibilities at Home:
  • What chores or household responsibilities does the applicant

have?

  • How does the applicant manage self-care responsibilities?
  • Is the applicant responsible for pet care or other household

tasks?

5. Fixated Interests For each identified interest:

  • Please describe the interest in detail.
  • How often does the applicant talk about or engage in this interest?
  • What is the typical duration of conversations or activities related

to this interest?

  • How intense is the interest? Does it exceed what would be expected

for the applicant’s age?

  • Is the interest unusually focused, repetitive, or preoccupying?
  • How does this interest impact daily functioning, including:
  • Academic participation
  • Family activities
  • Social interactions
  • Recreational activities

6. Social Impact of Fixated Interests

  • Does the applicant have difficulty shifting away from preferred

topics?

  • Does the applicant struggle to engage in reciprocal conversations?
  • Does the applicant tend to redirect conversations back to preferred

interests?

  • Do peers become disengaged or frustrated during conversations?
  • Do siblings or family members avoid conversations due to repetitive

discussion?

  • How do fixated interests impact friendship development and

maintenance?

  • Is the applicant able to participate in group activities, or does

the interest interfere?

  • Has the applicant experienced social isolation or challenges with

social connection as a result?

7. Attachment to Specific Objects For each object:

  • What is the object, and what is the nature of the attachment?
  • How does the attachment exceed developmental expectations?
  • How often does the applicant seek, carry, check on, or interact with

the object?

  • How intense is the attachment?
  • How does the applicant respond when the object is:
  • Unavailable
  • Misplaced
  • Removed
  • Inaccessible
  • Does the applicant exhibit distress, anxiety, emotional

dysregulation, behavioral challenges, repetitive questioning, or

seeking behaviors in these situations?

8. Impact Across Settings

  • Home:
  • How does the attachment or interest impact routines and

transitions?

  • Does it affect family activities or independence?
  • School:
  • Does it impact classroom participation, learning, transitions,

or behavior?

  • Community Activities:
  • Is there impact during outings or extracurricular activities?
  • Are accommodations or modifications needed?
  • Social Situations:
  • How does it affect peer interactions, social participation, and

flexibility during activities with others?

9. Accommodations and Supports

  • What accommodations are used to support access to preferred objects

or manage fixated interests?

  • Are replacement objects, visual supports, or transition supports

utilized?

  • How effective are these supports across different settings (home,

school, community)?

10. Remaining SCLARC Domains Continue with questions covering:

  • Psychiatric history
  • Substance use history
  • Education history
  • Work history (adults)
  • Legal history
  • Trauma history
  • Records reviewed/previous evaluations
  • Behavioral observations during interview/testing
  • Collateral/school observations (children)
  • Diagnostic substantiation (autism, ID, differential diagnosis)
  • Adaptive functioning and caregiver validity
  • Special considerations (court-dependency, parent consumers,

trauma/substance use, adults)

  • AI technology use disclosure (if applicable)
  • Recommendations

Note:

Responses should be documented with attention to discrepancies,

corroboration across sources, and clinical relevance to SCLARC

eligibility standards. Interviewers should probe for examples, clarify

ambiguous responses, and reconcile differences across informants and

records.

Do this. Write a practice intake plan for one make-believe child client and one adult client (which measures you would use, how you handle consent), following the SCLARC guidelines above, and go over it in supervision before you see real clients.

Reference · Ensora (TheraNest) Billing, Notes & Report Upload

Read this first. Getting billing right the first time saves the whole team from redoing it and keeps claims from being denied. Ensora is moving its old help articles, so old web links no longer work. Use the in-app Help (“?”) menu or search the TheraNest Community for any article by its title.

How psychological testing is billed

Testing is billed by time and by who did the work, added up across the whole case (usually several sessions). It is not billed as one session code.

  • Administration & scoring (giving and scoring the tests): by you or the psychologist — 96136 (first 30 min) plus 96137 (each extra 30 min); by a technician — 96138 / 96139; automated, software-scored — 96146 (per instrument).
  • Evaluation (putting the data together, interpreting, making clinical decisions, writing the report): 96130 (first hour) plus 96131 (each extra hour); for neuropsych — 96132 / 96133.
  • Brief screeners: 96127 (per instrument).
  • Units: the evaluation codes are billed per hour; the administration codes are billed per 30 minutes. The provider on the claim must have a current CPT license (you or your supervisor, per our billing setup).

Steps to submit a testing case

  1. Each session: open the chart, pick the right Case, and choose Add Note (Assessment/Testing type). Write down the tests you gave, your observations, and your time, split into the two groups (administration and evaluation). Note the exact start and stop times, and keep a running total. Sign within 48 hours.
  2. When the case is done, do the billing: open the Ledger, click New Invoice, and add one line for each CPT group with the right number of units (for example, 96136 + 96137 for administration; 96130 + 96131 for evaluation). Check the provider, place of service, and ICD-10 diagnosis. Save and submit the claim.
  3. Upload the report: go to Case, then Clinical Documents, then Upload Files. Reports are due within one week of finishing the assessment.
Example. 3 hours of administration and 2 hours of interpretation → 96136 ×1 and 96137 ×5 (administration), plus 96130 ×1 and 96131 ×1 (evaluation), each on its own invoice line.

Psychotherapy codes (quick reference)

90791 (intake) · 90832 (16–37 min) · 90834 (38–52 min) · 90837 (53+ min) · 90847 (family, client present) · 90846 (family, no client) · 90853 (group) · +90785 (interactive complexity). Count face-to-face time only; under 16 minutes is not billable. Record the exact start and stop times.

If you used the wrong code: do not fix a submitted claim yourself. Tell your supervisor or the billing partner, and they will correct it on the ledger.
